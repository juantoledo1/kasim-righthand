## Scale vs. Non-Scale: the paved road

Kasim opens by pushing back on the reflexive love of "scale":

> "People like to talk about scale. You know, 'I only want to do it if it scales.' I often wonder if those people even really know what the word scale means."

His definition — scale as a **paved road**:

> "Anything that scales, AI can do. I like to think of scale as a paved road. So you have this vehicle. Your business is the vehicle and you're trying to find a way from A to B and you are in the jungle, right? You're off-roading and you're running into barriers. You're trying to find a way to get through. And then at some point, you find what might be a dirt road that turns into an open road that turns into a paved road and then bam, you're off 100 miles an hour. You found scale."

The post-AI twist:

> "In a pre-AI world, that was still pretty possible. In a post-AI world, what's really interesting is all paved roads AI can traverse. Meaning if it scales, AI can do it."

The implication for entrepreneurs:

> "You, the entrepreneur, need to start looking for the things that don't scale."

The things that don't scale — and that he believes are the post-AI entrepreneurial unlock:

- Direct mail ("does not scale. At least not very well")
- Handwritten thank-you notes
- Calling individual clients to thank them
- Setting up at events / getting a booth
- Pounding the pavement, speaking, podcasting, knocking on doors, shaking hands, "kissing babies"

> "Those are the things that I believe in a post-AI world, that's going to be the entrepreneurial unlock."

## LinkedIn: from prospecting goldmine to AI slop

Kasim describes what good LinkedIn outreach used to be — researched, relevant, bespoke — and what AI has done to it:

> "You've already observed LinkedIn outreach. If you did it well, if you really researched somebody and had something interesting to say that was relevant to them and who they were, LinkedIn actually welcomed that type of behavior. I've had some really amazing experiences LinkedIn prospecting — until AI."

> "Now, my LinkedIn inbox is — I can't even tell you, it's garbage, because I just don't even look at it anymore. It's not worth looking at. There's nothing unique about it. There's nothing creative. There's nothing bespoke. There's nothing custom. And there's nobody in there that I think I'd want to talk to, because it's all AI slop."

The mechanism of the damage:

> "It's easy for everybody to do because it's automated and it scales. And so entrepreneurs took the thing that used to be really important and really helpful and really impactful and they decided, you know what, I'm just going to throw AI at this and I'm going to scale it to the moon. And in so doing, they ruin the damn thing."

## The surrogate question: Problem → Underlying Need → New Solution

Kasim then models exactly how he thinks past a dead channel (his Problem → Underlying Need → New Solution pattern):

> "The question becomes, what's the surrogate for LinkedIn outreach? Now that LinkedIn outreach has been burned to the ground by a bunch of evil marketers, of which I guess I am one. What was the itch that it was scratching? Because it's not that LinkedIn outreach was unique. It wasn't really a channel unto itself. It was just the most efficient way to allow business people to connect over shared interests."

The underlying need survives:

> "Do business people still want to connect over shared interests? Yes, of course they do. Obviously, they just don't want to be carpet bombed with a bunch of AI slop."

His answer points to **industry tribalism** — after "a massive shift or an extinction event," people "get real tribal" as a mechanism of safety:

> "What's the way that allows people in your industry to build meaningful connections? And I'm willing to bet it doesn't scale. I'm willing to bet it's something like going into an event, or showing up for the Zoom call, or participating in the forum."

## Give First, Give Last, Give More

His relationship-building law:

> "I always like to say, give first, give last, give more. If you want to build business, give first, give last, give more. Be the first one to give. Just show up giving. And then give more than anybody would expect that you would give. And then make sure that you're always the last one to give."

> "That evokes the law of reciprocity in a way that will blow your mind and change your life. But to do that, you have to first be in the room. And the room is a metaphor — I don't mean it has to be a physical room."

The non-scalable ways to get in the room:

> "Join a mastermind. Join a group. If you don't have one, launch a mastermind. Launch a group. Invite other people to get together. And it's crazy how ravenous folks are for this."

## The Daily Sai: a non-scalable experiment

His own proof point — a daily community room he started showing up for:

> "I started this group called the Daily Sai. I just started showing up every single day, every single business day at 3:00 p.m. Pacific. This was right before I get my kids from school. And I just opened a Zoom room and I would go live on my socials and I would invite anybody that wanted to join to join."

The result — and the cost:

> "It was crazy the number of people that would join consistently, that would join literally every single day. It blew my mind. I ended up being fatigued by it, to be honest with you. It was so powerful it wore me out. And that's what you want. Did it scale? No. Every single day for an hour at 3 p.m. Pacific does not scale by any means. And it was effective."

## The Dream 100

> "I think everybody in every business should have a dream 100 list. I don't remember who I learned this from. I wish I could give them credit. It's definitely not mine."

Definition and standard:

> "Your dream 100 is the list of 100 clients that you wish you could have — among all the other endeavors that you're already pursuing. You constantly work to approach your dream 100. Now, the odds of you working with those hundred are pretty slim, but your dream 100 should be so robust, so powerful that even if you only get one or two of them, it can change the face of your business."

> "And if the dream 100, by the way, isn't robust enough, then you're not thinking big enough."

### The septic company example (Customer → Organization → Multiplier)

To make the Dream 100 concrete, he walks through thinking bigger than the obvious customer:

> "Let's say that you own a septic company, right? I've owned a septic company in the past. You make your money pumping people's septic tank. One customer is worth one pump. Sometimes a customer can even be worth a whole septic tank replacement. But the value of a customer is pretty much capped at that."

Then the escalation:

- **HOA with a shared lift station** — every home on septic, sometimes one great big combined system: "there might be 300 homes in that community. And so that one customer brings you 300 contracts."
- **Manufacturing facilities** — they must have their own lift stations (industrial septic stations): "if I get a manufacturing customer, that's worth more than a big customer, but even that's not thinking big enough."
- **Property management companies** — "who are the property management companies that manage most of these manufacturing locations? Because that tends to be a property management problem. And it's such a unique specialization that only a handful of property managers are even capable of doing it."

> "Not only are you getting a bigger customer than your average residential customer — if you got the property management company instead of just the manufacturing company, you'd be getting a customer that feeds you more customers."

His universal claim: "You can do this in every industry — professional services are the easiest to point at, but you could have a food truck, be a dentist, be a traveling vacuum salesman."

## Pareto Talent: right hands, one in a thousand

Kasim applies the Dream 100 to his own business:

> "I own Pareto Talent with a business partner. We find the greatest people. We call them assistants — I hate that word. An assistant gets your coffee and checks your emails. We call them right hands."

What makes a Pareto right hand:

> "They're AI enabled, meaning they're trained on AI to a degree that I think should blow your mind. It takes a thousand applicants to produce one. And when I place these right hands with entrepreneurs, they change lives. I see that all the time, over and over and over again. They're the best people in the whole world."

The Pareto Dream 100: not individual clients, but **people with communities**:

> "What me and my business partner did is we sat down and we thought, who are the 100 contacts that we would want in our circle that could change the face of our business? And every single person represented by this little stack that I'm building has their own community."

## The pitch inside the envelopes

The sales letter to each Dream 100 contact, summarized in his own words:

> "I think that your community needs an army of people prepared to help them who know AI, but more importantly who know your content, your information, your training, your way of approaching things, your paradigm, what you teach — because everybody with a community is an educator."

The offer: train assistants not just on the standard curriculum but on everything that community needs — "it doesn't matter what it is: you have a community of accountants, CFOs, I'm going to train my assistants on everything a CFO would need — annual reporting requirements, why April's do-or-die time, semantic indexing, jargon, nomenclature, specific software" — and then a profit share with the community owner:

> "What if when you have a community, if you refer my people to your community, you and I do a profit share? Costs you nothing. I help your community. I make you look great. I probably help with your retention. And you get a little shot in the pocket monthly for the life of our relationship."

The strategic point — this beats placing "one person at a time":

> "Right now we place assistants one person at a time, but every now and again I get somebody in my circle — and we have a handful now — who is able to move more units. So why wouldn't I pursue that process to a greater degree?"

## Doing the non-scalable thing yourself

> "The way to do it is to do that which does not scale. What I'm doing right now, which you've been watching me do for the last however long I've been recording, this is a non-scalable endeavor."

He acknowledges it is "beneath [his] pay grade" — he had no intention of coating letters — but noticed that "when you create content while you're doing things, it just makes people interested for whatever reason." He had already done his dishes and laundry, saved this task for his home helper, then decided to figure out how long it takes himself. "I think it was a pretty effective use of my time. You may disagree."

The closing argument:

> "Go find the thing in your business that doesn't scale, but that can still change the face of the business. There are relationships in this little stack that could literally 10x my business. So even if I didn't have a home helper, and even if I had to sit here stuffing envelopes all by myself, wouldn't it make sense to do that if there's a better than zero chance of potentially 10xing my business?"

He closes: "That doesn't mean that you're going to do this every single day of your life. But I do think that things like this are valuable use of your time when given the opportunity."
